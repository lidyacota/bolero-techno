About

Bolero Techno

Abraham Truxillo, a Mexican poet, is my husband. For fourteen years I have watched his work come into being, and I knew some of the poems in Bolero Techno before the book was published in 2025. His poems can be read like the tracks of an album: several titles use the names of musical genres (folk, lullaby, madrigal, requiem, bolero, ballad, dub), and the book includes a section called “Bonus track.” When it came out, I asked myself a question: what happens to a poem when it is heard instead of read? I did not know the answer, and that uncertainty was the reason to look for it.

My field is technology. I am a specialist in software and information technology, with experience in artificial intelligence and machine learning. Because of that background, the question took the form of an experiment: to set the poems to music with AI and turn the book into a work of electronic literature.

Before starting, I asked Abraham for permission. I warned him that I did not know what the result would be, that I needed complete freedom, and that I might take his poems into musical genres he would not like, including some of my guilty pleasures. He agreed. I take responsibility for the musical decisions in this project.

In May 2025 I worked through the poems in the book one by one and created a song for each poem. I kept the songs that gave the most interesting results; curiously, that turned out to be almost all of them: twenty-seven. Then I turned each song into a video to share it on YouTube. I did not know whether it would work. It worked in a way I did not expect: the music changed the way I read the poems.

On a first reading, “Réquiem del ahogado” (Requiem for the Drowned Man) sounded to me like a lament: a song of mourning, deeply melancholic, like the one a catastrophe leaves behind. That tone immediately brought to mind “La Llorona.” That is why I chose the traditional music of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec for the poem. After listening to it several times, I understood a line I had read without pausing: “Qué triste nos miraba la boca del ahogado” (How sadly the drowned man’s mouth looked at us).

In “Semblanza” (Profile), the poet presents himself as the cover of his own book, boasts of being “number two on the list of emerging authors,” and announces himself: “Coming soon on Amazon!” On the page, the irony is subtle. With a big band, that pose becomes a spectacle and the self-portrait is revealed as self-promotion. The rhythm made what was already in the text even more evident.

When you listen to these poems with their music, the same will probably happen to you. There are lines that may not move you when you read them, or not entirely; when you hear them sung, they can reach you with a new emotion and form images in you that you had not seen. Then you will know what I mean.

The same medicine can reach the body by different routes: the powder of a pill or the liquid of an injection. The substance does not change; the route does, and with it the speed and intensity with which it acts. I discovered something similar with these poems: the words are the same, but music opens another way in, and they arrive sooner and go deeper, to where we keep what we know and what we have lived. I began wanting to listen to the poems and ended up reading them better, surprised by the stature of a poetry I place alongside the great avant-garde poets I admire.

In October 2026 I designed and built this website to bring together this work of poetry set to music. Each poem appears in full, in its original Spanish, with the spacing and indentation Abraham gave it on the page, next to its song and the AI prompt used to generate the music. That way, visitors can read and listen to the poems at the same time, in a contemporary, sensory, and experimental poetic experience.

Bolero Techno takes a poetry book already in print, by a living author and with his approval, and transforms it, through AI music direction and web design, into an experience of electronic literature. In 2026 it was selected for the tenth edition of El Aleph. Festival de Arte y Ciencia at UNAM, which, under the theme New Narratives, reflected on the challenges of the present, among them the role of artificial intelligence.

Bolero Techno is an artistic, literary, musical, and digital project. It explores hybrid formats and languages and the fusion of word and sound, and it seeks new forms of poetic expression in the digital environment. It belongs to electronic literature. Bringing a poem into music is what semiotics calls an intersemiotic translation: the passage from one sign system to another, from writing to sound. At that crossroads of poetry and music, the work belongs to the musicalization of poetry and AI poetry; to sound poetry, electronic poetry or electropoetry, videopoetry, and Latin American spoken word; to digital poetry, experimental poetry, and futurist poetry; and to hybrid art, digital art, and the relationship between artificial intelligence and art, and between literature, technology, and emotion. It is also part of Mexican and Latin American digital literature made by women.

Lidya Cota, Mexico City, 2026

Lidya Cota

Mexican specialist in software and information technology, with experience in technological innovation, artificial intelligence, and machine learning. She brings together programming and the design of digital tools with the creation of interactive experiences, and promotes cultural outreach and digital storytelling from both a technical and an artistic perspective.

www.lidyacota.com

Abraham Truxillo

Mexican poet, essayist, and fiction writer, born in 1983. He holds a degree in Hispanic Literature from UNAM. Author of Postales del ventrílocuo (2011), Huracán. Antipoema (2025), Bestiario marino (2025), and Bolero Techno (2025).
